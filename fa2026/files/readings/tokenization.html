Handout — Tokenization & Subwords

Session number TBD  ·  Course page

On this page

Every handout so far has quietly assumed that text arrives as a sequence of tokens from a finite vocabulary $\Sigma$. Nothing in the world hands you that. Between a UTF-8 file and $x_1, \dots, x_T$ sits a design decision — and it is the one decision in the whole pipeline that is made once, frozen before training, and then silently constrains everything the model can ever do.

1The gap between text and the model

Handout #2 opened with a vocabulary $\Sigma$ and a distribution over $\Sigma^*$, and then did what every intro does: line.split(). Splitting on whitespace is a tokenizer, and a bad one. This section is about why every obvious choice fails, because the reason subword tokenization looks so strange is that it is the only thing left standing.

1.1  Words do not work

Take $\Sigma$ to be the words of English. Three problems, in increasing order of seriousness.

The vocabulary is unbounded. Not large — unbounded. Every new proper noun, product name, typo, hashtag, URL and inflection is a new word, and the count of distinct words in a corpus keeps climbing as the corpus grows; it never saturates. You therefore have to truncate, and the truncation point is arbitrary.

Every truncation creates an <UNK>. Handout #2 §3.3 introduced this as the fix for unseen words. Look at what it actually costs. A model with <UNK> in its vocabulary is a model that can be surprised in a way it cannot represent: at generation time it may emit <UNK>, which is not a word, and at scoring time it assigns one shared probability to the entire infinite set of things it has never seen. Worse, that makes perplexity gameable — shrink your vocabulary and more of the test set collapses into a single high-probability <UNK> event, and your number improves while your model gets less useful.

The vocabulary is where the sparsity lives. The output layer is a $d \times |\Sigma|$ matrix and the softmax runs over $|\Sigma|$ entries, so a 500k-word vocabulary is expensive on both counts. And rare words — the ones that carry the most information — get the fewest gradient updates, which is Handout #2 §3.3's counting problem reappearing in a neural model. Zipf's law guarantees that most of your vocabulary is rare.

And that is before you leave English "Word" is not a well-defined unit. Chinese and Japanese do not delimit words with spaces at all. Turkish and Finnish are agglutinative: a single "word" can encode what English needs a clause for, so the number of distinct word forms explodes. German compounds without spaces. A tokenizer whose primitive is "the thing between two spaces" has quietly assumed a language family.

1.2  Characters do not work either

So go the other way: let $\Sigma$ be characters. Every problem above vanishes. $|\Sigma|$ is tiny, nothing is ever out-of-vocabulary, and there is no <UNK>. The cost moves somewhere else.

Sequences get roughly 4–5× longer. That is not a constant-factor annoyance — it multiplies through everything downstream. A recurrent model has to carry information across 5× more steps to relate two adjacent words. A Transformer's attention is quadratic in sequence length, so 5× longer is 25× the attention cost for the same text. And the model now spends most of its capacity learning spelling: predicting e after th is nearly free information, and the loss is dominated by these easy positions.

WordsCharactersSubwords
$|\Sigma|$unbounded → truncated to ~50k–500k~100 (or 256 bytes)chosen, typically 32k–256k
Out-of-vocabularyalways possiblenevernever
Sequence lengthshortest~4–5× longer~1.3× words
Rare-word learningpoor (few updates)n/a — built from partsgood (shared parts)
Morphologyinvisible (run/running unrelated)must be learnedpartly explicit

The last row is the one that is easy to miss. With a word vocabulary, run and running are two unrelated integers; whatever the model learns about one, it must learn again for the other. Subwords make the shared part literally the same token, so the parameters are shared for free.

2UTF-8, and why bytes are the floor

Modern tokenizers do not start from characters. They start from bytes, and the reason is worth stating carefully because it is the design decision that makes <UNK> impossible rather than merely unlikely.

Unicode has ~150,000 assigned code points and grows with each release, so "character" is not a fixed alphabet either. UTF-8 encodes any code point as 1–4 bytes, and there are exactly $256$ possible byte values, forever. Take $\Sigma_0 = \{0, 1, \dots, 255\}$ and every possible text — every language, every emoji, every corrupted file — is already in $\Sigma_0^*$. There is nothing left to be out of vocabulary.

The price is paid unevenly, and this is the part students should see as numbers rather than as a remark: 

for s in ["hello", "héllo", "こんにちは", "مرحبا", "🙂"]:
    print(f"{s!r:12} chars={len(s):3} bytes={len(s.encode()):3}")

# 'hello'        chars=  5 bytes=  5
# 'héllo'        chars=  5 bytes=  6
# 'こんにちは'      chars=  5 bytes= 15
# 'مرحبا'        chars=  5 bytes= 10
# '🙂'            chars=  1 bytes=  4

ASCII is one byte per character; Japanese is three; a single emoji is four. Before any merging happens at all, the same content costs three times as many symbols in Japanese as in English. §4.3 returns to what that means for users.

Definition A byte-level tokenizer is one whose base vocabulary is the 256 byte values, so that any input is representable and <UNK> never occurs. A subword tokenizer additionally learns a set of multi-byte units from a corpus, to buy back the sequence length that bytes cost. Almost every current model is both.

3Byte-pair encoding

BPE was a data-compression algorithm from 1994, repurposed for NLP in 2015. The idea is one sentence: repeatedly find the most frequent adjacent pair of symbols and replace it with a single new symbol. Each replacement costs one vocabulary slot and shortens every occurrence by one. Run it $k$ times and you have a vocabulary of size $256 + k$ that is adapted to your corpus.

3.1  Training: merge the most frequent pair

Here is a real run on a four-sentence corpus, with $k = 6$ merges. This is genuine output, not an illustration: 

text = ("the cat sat on the mat. the cat sat on the hat. "
        "the rat sat on the mat. the bat sat on the cat.")

tokenizer = BasicTokenizer()
tokenizer.train(text, vocab_size=256 + 6, verbose=True)

# merge 1/6: (97, 116)  -> 256 (b'at')    had 12 occurrences
# merge 2/6: (116, 104) -> 257 (b'th')    had  8 occurrences
# merge 3/6: (257, 101) -> 258 (b'the')   had  8 occurrences
# merge 4/6: (258, 32)  -> 259 (b'the ')  had  8 occurrences
# merge 5/6: (256, 32)  -> 260 (b'at ')   had  8 occurrences
# merge 6/6: (32, 259)  -> 261 (b' the ') had  7 occurrences

Read it slowly, because four separate lessons are visible in six lines.

  • Merges compose. Merge 3 consumes token 257, which merge 2 created. The units are built up in a fixed order, and each new token is a tree over earlier ones — the is (th)+e is ((t+h))+e.
  • It found morphology without being told about it. at and the are real units of English. Nobody supplied a dictionary; frequency alone produced them.
  • Space is just byte 32. Merges 4, 5 and 6 all involve it, so the  (with trailing space) and  the  are tokens. This is why tokens in real vocabularies have leading spaces attached, and it is also a pathology: a token spanning a word boundary means the model sees "cat" and " cat" as unrelated integers. Production tokenizers restrict merges with a pre-tokenization regex to stop exactly this; §5 shows where minbpe chose not to.
  • It is greedy and irreversible. Merge 1 was taken because 12 > 8, and nothing later reconsiders it. There is no search and no optimality claim.
The order is the model The output of training is not a set of tokens, it is an ordered list of merge rules. Two tokenizers with identical vocabularies but different merge orders will segment text differently. This is why you cannot mix a checkpoint with the wrong tokenizer version and why the merge list ships with the weights.

3.2  Encoding: replay the merges, in order

To tokenize new text: convert to bytes, then apply the learned merges repeatedly, always choosing the merge that was learned earliest, until none apply. Earliest-first is what makes encoding consistent with training. 

tokenizer.encode("the cat sat")
# [259, 99, 260, 115, 256]
#   ^     ^    ^    ^    ^
# 'the ' 'c'  'at ' 's'  'at'

# 11 bytes in, 5 tokens out; and it round-trips:
assert tokenizer.decode(tokenizer.encode("the cat sat")) == "the cat sat"

Note that the segmentation is nothing a linguist would choose — 'c', 'at ', 's', 'at' cuts "cat" and "sat" in half. It is not supposed to be linguistically principled. It is supposed to be short, deterministic, and total, and it is all three.

3.3  Decoding, and why it can fail

Decoding is the easy direction: look up the bytes for each id, concatenate, and interpret the result as UTF-8. The subtlety is that the model emits token ids one at a time, and there is no guarantee a prefix of them forms valid UTF-8 — a multi-byte character can be cut in half. Real code therefore decodes with errors="replace": 

tokenizer.decode([104, 101, 196])   # 'he�'  -- 196 begins a 2-byte char that never finishes

That � is not a bug in the tokenizer; it is a truthful report that the model produced half a character. If you have ever seen a streaming chat interface emit a replacement character that resolves itself a moment later, you have watched this happen: the second byte arrived on the next token.

Exercise 3.1 — merge by hand

Run two merges of BPE by hand on aaabdaaabac (treat each letter as one byte). Give the merge rules and the final sequence.

Answer
Pair counts: aa appears 4 times (positions 1–2, 2–3, 6–7, 7–8), ab 2, ba 2, bd 1, da 1, ac 1. So the first merge is aa → X. Applying it left to right: aaabdaaabac → XabdXabac. Now Xa appears twice and ab twice — a tie, broken by whichever the implementation's max encounters first. Taking ab → Y gives XYdXYac; taking Xa → Y gives YbdYbac. Both are legitimate BPE runs, which is the point of the exercise: tie-breaking is implementation-defined, and it is one of several reasons two "BPE tokenizers" trained on the same text can disagree.

Note also that aa in aaa was counted twice but could only be merged once — see §6 Q3.

4What your tokenizer does to everything downstream

Tokenization is usually taught as preprocessing, which makes it sound harmless. It is not preprocessing; it is a choice of what the model's atoms are. Three consequences, the first of which will affect how you read every number in this course.

4.1  Perplexity is not comparable across tokenizers

Handout #2 §5.1 defined perplexity as $\exp\!\big(\tfrac{1}{N}\sum_{t} -\log p(x_t \mid x_{<t})\big)$ — an average per token. If two models tokenize the same text into different numbers of tokens, they are dividing the same total by different $N$.

Make it concrete. One test string; two tokenizers; suppose both models assign the identical total log-probability $-30$ nats to the string — that is, they are equally good models of the text: 

import math
total_logp = -30.0            # nats, for the whole 40-byte string

for name, n_tokens in [("byte-level", 40), ("BPE", 12)]:
    per_token = -total_logp / n_tokens
    print(f"{name:11} {n_tokens:3} tokens  loss {per_token:.2f} nats  ppl {math.exp(per_token):6.2f}")

# byte-level   40 tokens  loss 0.75 nats  ppl   2.12
# BPE          12 tokens  loss 2.50 nats  ppl  12.18

Perplexity 2.12 versus 12.18 for two models that are exactly as good. The byte-level model looks six times better and is not. Nothing is wrong with the definition; the quantity is simply per-token, and "token" is not a fixed unit.

What is comparable Bits per byte (or per character): divide the total log-probability by the number of bytes in the text rather than the number of tokens. $\text{bpb} = \frac{-\sum_t \log_2 p(x_t \mid x_{<t})}{\#\text{bytes}}$. The numerator is a property of the model and the text; the denominator is a property of the text alone. In the example above both models give $30 / (\ln 2 \cdot 40) = 1.082$ bits per byte — equal, as they should be. When you see two papers' perplexities compared, check that they share a tokenizer. Often they do not.

4.2  Failures that are really tokenizer failures

A surprising number of famous LLM weaknesses are not reasoning failures at all. They are consequences of the segmentation the model was handed.

  • Spelling and character counting. Asked how many rs are in a word, the model does not see letters — it sees two or three opaque integers. The letters are recoverable only from what it has learned about those integers. This is genuinely hard for the same reason it would be hard for you to count the letters in a word you only ever heard spoken.
  • Reversing strings — same cause. The atoms are not the units the task asks about.
  • Arithmetic. Whether 1234 is one token, or 123+4, or 12+34, depends on which digit strings were frequent in the tokenizer's training corpus. Column-wise addition is nearly impossible when the columns are not aligned with the atoms. Several model families now force digits to be tokenized individually, purely to fix this — a tokenizer change made to buy a reasoning capability.
  • The trailing-space bug. Because  cat and cat are different tokens (§3.1), a prompt ending in a space puts the model in a different state than one that does not, and can measurably degrade output. This is a real, reported, confusing-in-practice bug with a purely tokenizer-shaped cause.
Why this matters for how you evaluate When a model fails a task, the useful first question is not "does it lack the concept?" but "can it even see the units the task is about?" These two hypotheses call for completely different fixes, and confusing them wastes a lot of time.

4.3  The multilingual tax

§2 showed that Japanese text costs ~3× the bytes of English. Merges are learned from a corpus, so a corpus that is mostly English spends its vocabulary budget on English units, and the ~3× byte disadvantage is not bought back by merging. The result, measured across current tokenizers, is that the same content can take several times more tokens in a lower-resource language than in English. That means, for the same text: a larger share of a fixed context window, more compute per document, higher cost per API call, and — since attention is quadratic — disproportionately more work. An implementation detail chosen for compression turns into a distributional question about who the system serves well.

5Reading real code: minbpe

Everything in §3 is about 30 lines of Python. Karpathy's minbpe is the clean version of it, and the file to read is basic.py: byte-level BPE with no regex pre-tokenization and no special tokens, which is exactly the algorithm above and nothing else.

Try it

Open these beside this page:

  • minbpe/base.py — the two helpers get_stats and merge, plus the save/load format. The helpers are the whole of BPE's arithmetic.

5.1  Two helpers, and that is the arithmetic

base.py · get_stats, merge (docstrings trimmed)

def get_stats(ids, counts=None):
    counts = {} if counts is None else counts
    for pair in zip(ids, ids[1:]): # iterate consecutive elements
        counts[pair] = counts.get(pair, 0) + 1
    return counts

def merge(ids, pair, idx):
    newids = []
    i = 0
    while i < len(ids):
        # if not at the very last position AND the pair matches, replace it
        if ids[i] == pair[0] and i < len(ids) - 1 and ids[i+1] == pair[1]:
            newids.append(idx)
            i += 2
        else:
            newids.append(ids[i])
            i += 1
    return newids

zip(ids, ids[1:]) is the idiom for "all adjacent pairs" and is worth adding to your vocabulary. merge is a left-to-right scan with a two-step skip on a match: the i += 2 is what prevents a merged token from being immediately re-merged with the byte after it. Both functions operate on flat lists of int — there is no tree, no trie, no string anywhere in the hot path.

One asymmetry between them is a genuine subtlety rather than a bug, and §6 Q3 asks about it: get_stats([1,1,1]) reports {(1,1): 2}, but merge([1,1,1], (1,1), 4) returns [4, 1] — one merge, not two.

5.2  The training loop

basic.py · BasicTokenizer.train (verbatim; the verbose print and the two closing self. assignments elided)

def train(self, text, vocab_size, verbose=False):
    assert vocab_size >= 256
    num_merges = vocab_size - 256

    # input text preprocessing
    text_bytes = text.encode("utf-8") # raw bytes
    ids = list(text_bytes) # list of integers in range 0..255

    # iteratively merge the most common pairs to create new tokens
    merges = {} # (int, int) -> int
    vocab = {idx: bytes([idx]) for idx in range(256)} # int -> bytes
    for i in range(num_merges):
        # count up the number of times every consecutive pair appears
        stats = get_stats(ids)
        # find the pair with the highest count
        pair = max(stats, key=stats.get)
        # mint a new token: assign it the next available id
        idx = 256 + i
        # replace all occurrences of pair in ids with idx
        ids = merge(ids, pair, idx)
        # save the merge
        merges[pair] = idx
        vocab[idx] = vocab[pair[0]] + vocab[pair[1]]

Read the loop body against §3.1 and it is a line-for-line match: count, take the argmax, mint the next id, rewrite the sequence, record the rule. Four things to notice.

  • assert vocab_size >= 256 and num_merges = vocab_size - 256: the 256 bytes are not negotiable, so the only free parameter is how many merges you can afford. "Vocabulary size 32768" means 32512 merges.
  • vocab[idx] = vocab[pair[0]] + vocab[pair[1]] — plain bytes concatenation. Every token's meaning is defined recursively by the two tokens it came from, which is why base.py can rebuild the entire vocabulary from the merge list alone (_build_vocab) and why the saved model file contains only merges.
  • max(stats, key=stats.get) silently picks the first maximum on a tie, in dict insertion order. That is the implementation-defined tie-break from Exercise 3.1, and it is one line.
  • It is $O(k \cdot n)$: a full pass over the whole sequence per merge. Fine for a tutorial, hopeless for a real corpus, which is why production trainers maintain incremental counts. Notice that get_stats's optional counts argument is the seam left for exactly that.

5.3  encode: replay in merge order

basic.py · BasicTokenizer.encode (verbatim)

def encode(self, text):
    # given a string text, return the token ids
    text_bytes = text.encode("utf-8") # raw bytes
    ids = list(text_bytes) # list of integers in range 0..255
    while len(ids) >= 2:
        # find the pair with the lowest merge index
        stats = get_stats(ids)
        pair = min(stats, key=lambda p: self.merges.get(p, float("inf")))
        # subtle: if there are no more merges available, the key will
        # result in an inf for every single pair, and the min will be
        # just the first pair in the list, arbitrarily
        # we can detect this terminating case by a membership check
        if pair not in self.merges:
            break # nothing else can be merged anymore
        # otherwise let's merge the best pair (lowest merge index)
        idx = self.merges[pair]
        ids = merge(ids, pair, idx)
    return ids

This is the single most instructive function in the file, because the obvious implementation is wrong and the code shows why.

min, not max. Training used the most frequent pair; encoding uses the earliest-learned pair, because self.merges[p] is the id, and ids were minted in order. Frequency is irrelevant now — it was already used, once, at training time. Replaying in the original order is what guarantees new text is segmented the way the training text was.

The float("inf") default and the membership check. Pairs with no merge rule get infinite priority, so they sort last. But if every pair is unmergeable, min still returns something — the first pair, arbitrarily — and merging it would corrupt the output. Hence the explicit if pair not in self.merges: break. The four-line comment in the source is there because the author knew this looked redundant and is not. Sentinel-value tricks almost always need a companion check like this; it is a pattern worth recognizing.

5.4  What the file deliberately does not do

The module docstring is unusually valuable, because it tells you the shape of the gap between this and a production tokenizer:

basic.py · module docstring (verbatim)

"""
Minimal (byte-level) Byte Pair Encoding tokenizer.

Algorithmically follows along the GPT tokenizer:
https://github.com/openai/gpt-2/blob/master/src/encoder.py

But:
- Does not handle the regular expression splitting pattern.
- Does not handle any special tokens.
"""

Those two omissions are exactly the two pathologies you already met. No regex splitting is why b' the ' became a token in §3.1 — GPT's tokenizer first splits text into word-ish chunks and only merges within a chunk, so no token can span a word boundary. No special tokens means there is no <|endoftext|>, and therefore no way to mark a document boundary — the thing Handout #2 §1.2 needed </s> for. The sibling file regex.py adds both, and reading it afterwards is a good exercise in seeing how much complexity those two features cost.

5.5  Now do something to it

Q1. Clone it and run the §3.1 example yourself with verbose=True, then raise vocab_size to 256+20. At what point do the merges stop being interesting, and why?

Answer
On a 96-byte corpus the useful merges are exhausted almost immediately: once the frequent units are absorbed, the most-frequent remaining pair has a count of 2 or even 1, and BPE starts minting tokens for whole sentences — memorizing the corpus rather than learning units of the language. This is the tokenizer version of overfitting, and it tells you the real constraint: vocabulary size only makes sense relative to corpus size. Real tokenizers are trained on gigabytes precisely so that the 30,000th merge is still supported by many occurrences.

Q2. encode calls get_stats over the entire sequence on every iteration of the while loop. What is the complexity, and what happens if you call encode on a 1 MB string?

Answer
Each iteration is $O(n)$ for the scan plus $O(n)$ for the rewrite, and the loop runs once per applicable merge — up to $O(k)$ where $k$ is the vocabulary's merge count. So worst case $O(k n)$, with $k \approx 30{,}000$ in practice: a 1 MB string is minutes-to-hours, not milliseconds. The fix real implementations use is to chunk the input first (the regex pre-tokenization from §5.4 does this as a side effect — no merge can cross a chunk, so you encode each short chunk independently and can cache results per chunk). Two features for the price of one, which is usually why an "obvious cleanup" in someone else's code turns out to be load-bearing.

Q3. Explain the get_stats/merge asymmetry from §5.1: counting says (1,1) occurs twice in [1,1,1], merging produces only one new token. Is train wrong?

Answer
Not wrong, but the counts are an upper bound on the merges a rule will actually perform, because overlapping occurrences compete and the left-to-right scan with i += 2 takes the leftmost. So max(stats, key=stats.get) is greedy on a slightly optimistic estimate, and on inputs with long runs of a repeated byte it can pick a pair that yields fewer merges than a competitor. The consequence is a marginally worse compression ratio, not incorrect behavior — encode and decode stay exact inverses regardless. Worth knowing because it is the kind of "close enough" approximation that real systems are full of, and it is invisible unless you compare the two helpers.

Q4. Add a special token. Sketch what you would have to change so that <|endoftext|> is a single id that can never be produced by merging.

Answer
Reserve an id above the merge range and register it in self.special_tokens (base.py already has the field and _build_vocab already handles it). The real work is in encode: you must split the input on the literal special-token strings first, BPE-encode only the segments between them, and splice the reserved ids in. If instead you let the special text flow through the byte path, it gets merged into ordinary tokens and becomes indistinguishable from a user typing <|endoftext|> — which is precisely the injection vector behind a class of prompt-boundary attacks. Note also the security asymmetry this creates: whether user text is allowed to name special tokens is a decision, and the safe answer is no. See regex.py's allowed_special parameter for how the library exposes that choice.

Q5. Where in this file is the decision that makes <UNK> impossible?

Answer
Two lines, both in train: text_bytes = text.encode("utf-8") and vocab = {idx: bytes([idx]) for idx in range(256)}. The base vocabulary is all 256 bytes and the input is always bytes, so every possible input is representable before a single merge is learned. Merges can only ever shorten a sequence, never make it unrepresentable. That is the whole of §2 in two lines, and it is why no modern tokenizer has an <UNK> token — the problem Handout #2 §3.3 needed one for was designed out of existence rather than solved.

6Check yourself

Q1. Model A has perplexity 8.2, model B has 21.5, on the same test file. Which is better?

Answer
Unanswerable as stated. If A is byte-level and B uses a 50k subword vocabulary, B is probably the far better model — B's tokens each carry much more content, so a higher per-token perplexity is expected. You need either a shared tokenizer or a tokenizer-independent measure such as bits per byte. §4.1.

Q2. True or false: a byte-level BPE tokenizer with a 32k vocabulary can fail to represent some input strings.

Answer
False, and the reason is structural rather than statistical: the 256 single-byte tokens are always present, so the worst case for an unfamiliar string is that it is encoded as many short tokens — never that it cannot be encoded. Contrast a word-level vocabulary, where the worst case is <UNK> and information is genuinely destroyed. §2, §5.5 Q5.

Q3. Your model scores 1.9 bits per byte on English and 1.9 on Japanese. Is it equally good at both?

Answer
Equally good per byte, which is not equally good per unit of content: Japanese needs about three times the bytes for the same information (§2), so equal bits-per-byte means it is spending roughly three times the bits to say the same thing. Bits per byte removes the tokenizer from the comparison but not the encoding, and no scalar fully fixes cross-language comparison. Being explicit about which denominator you chose, and why, is the actual skill.

Q4. Why does adding merges to a tokenizer reduce sequence length but increase the size of the model's embedding and output layers? Which cost dominates?

Answer
Each merge adds a vocabulary entry, and both the embedding matrix and the output projection are $d \times |\Sigma|$, so parameters grow linearly in $|\Sigma|$. Meanwhile sequence length falls — with diminishing returns, since later merges are rarer and save less. For a Transformer, the compute per layer is roughly linear in $T$ and the attention term quadratic in $T$, so shortening sequences is worth a great deal; the softmax over $|\Sigma|$ is the main countervailing cost and it is why vocabularies sit in the tens of thousands rather than the millions. The trade-off is genuinely empirical, which is why "vocabulary size" is a hyperparameter and not a theorem.

Q5. You fine-tune a pretrained model on a corpus of chemistry papers. Someone proposes retraining the tokenizer on that corpus first, to get better units for chemical names. What breaks?

Answer
Every pretrained weight that is indexed by token id: the embedding matrix and the output layer become meaningless, since id 4,712 no longer refers to what it did during pretraining. You would be discarding the pretraining you were trying to build on. This is why tokenizers are frozen for the life of a model family, and why extending a vocabulary (appending new ids with freshly initialized rows, leaving existing ids untouched) is the practical move instead. §3.1's callout — the merge list ships with the weights — is the same fact from the other side.

Q6. A colleague reports that their model's perplexity dropped from 30 to 12 after they "cleaned up the tokenizer." What is the first thing you ask?

Answer
Whether the token count on the test set changed — because if the tokenizer changed, the denominator changed and the two numbers measure different things (§4.1). A tokenizer change that merges more aggressively will lower token count, raise per-token loss and raise perplexity; one that adds <UNK>-like collapsing will lower it for free. Ask for bits per byte, or for perplexity under a fixed reference tokenizer. This is not pedantry: it is one of the easiest ways to accidentally publish an improvement that is not one.

  • minbpe/regex.py — the sibling of the file we read, adding the two things §5.4 says basic.py lacks. Read it as a diff against basic.py.
  • Kudo & Richardson, SentencePiece (2018) — the other major family, including the unigram LM tokenizer, which picks a segmentation by likelihood rather than by greedy merging.
